Benchmark · October 2026

Four decision models, 679 labeled cases

Jev, OpenAI Decisions, Cloudflare Clef and Clef-flash answered the same 1,904 questions over 679 labeled cases: the original 300 template-built cases plus 379 new cases drawn from real HuggingFace corpora and hand-written invoices. This page reports accuracy, calibration, latency and cost for each, with the statistical checks behind every comparison.

Calibration gap is how far "90% sure" is from being right 90% of the time; 0.03 is honest, 0.15 is bluffing. Harder cases roughly tripled every model's gap versus the first round (then 0.03–0.07, now 0.08–0.10), with Clef the least degraded. ≥90% sure and wrong is the dangerous mistake: rarest for Flash at three in a hundred, most common for OpenAI at seven. Accuracy at 80% automation: if you auto-act on the 80% most confident answers and send the rest to a human, this is how often the automated part is right.

Speed

The typical wait, and the bad-day wait your timeout has to cover.

OpenAI has the fastest typical call but its worst 5% now stretches to 2.9 seconds (a few slow outlier calls on the new cases). Jev is still the most predictable: its 95th percentile sits 65 ms above its median. No HTTP errors or timeouts for any model across 2,716 calls.

Is the gap real?

With 1,904 questions, small gaps can still be luck. We re-shuffled the results 2,000 times; if the 95% range crosses zero, call it a tie.

Three verdicts, no other wording: a range that stays on one side of zero is a real gap; one that crosses zero is too close to call. The p-values are McNemar tests on the cases where the two models disagreed (Jev versus OpenAI disagreed on 230 of 1,904 questions, OpenAI right on 143). Agreement runs 79 to 88% across pairs; Flash agrees least with the others.

Method

What was tested, how, and what to be careful about.

Setup

  • 679 cases across five domains: support triage, invoice routing, security incident severity, tool-call versus clarify, and an out-of-scope guardrail set. Each case carries one to three questions of three types: yes/no, choice, and a 1–4 score. The first 300 cases were template-built; the 379 new cases (data/gold.v2.jsonl, built by data/build_gold_v2.py) use real text: Banking77 customer queries, CLINC150 out-of-scope queries, When2Call tool decisions, SOC logs, plus 55 hand-written invoices.
  • Gold labels for new cases were written by a single labeler from independent source labels (never from model output) plus an audited sample, and tagged clear, borderline, ambiguous or out-of-scope. 49 new cases are paraphrases of another new case with the same gold.
  • Same payload to every model: state plus questions. OpenAI Decisions gets the predicate/choice/score translation and a stringified state, since it rejects object states.
  • Protocol: seeded shuffle, models run round-robin per case to balance time of day, 1 request per second, 60 s timeout, retries only on 429/5xx. Latency is client-measured. Cost is computed from each response's reported usage at list price.
  • Stats: paired bootstrap with 2,000 resamples for accuracy gaps, McNemar on disagreements, n reported everywhere.

Caveats

  • One set of labels, one labeler. The 379 new gold labels were written by a single labeler (the author) from independent source labels plus an audit pass; there was no second labeler. Uncertain rows were tiered borderline/ambiguous rather than clear, and per-slice reporting is the mitigation.
  • Public sources. The new texts come from public HuggingFace corpora the models may have seen in training. All four models faced identical text, so the comparison stays fair, but absolute scores on those slices may overstate real-world accuracy.
  • Sample size. 679 cases settled Jev versus OpenAI, but Clef remains statistically tied with both leaders; ranking all three with confidence would need more data still.
  • Text only. Clef and OpenAI accept images, Jev does not, so images were excluded to keep the comparison fair.
  • Pricing uses published list prices. Clef-flash's input rate was ambiguous in the docs; the $0.09 per million figure was used.
